The Society's argument for AI governance begins with a question about purpose.

Why should civilization be governed at all? What makes one arrangement of institutions better than another? What should greater intelligence help us accomplish?

Our answer is human flourishing. We do not regard the preservation of human political authority, the victory of a coalition, or the endurance of a familiar administrative structure as an end in itself.

## Institutions are means

An institution has a history, procedures, and a way of describing its own importance. Those features can become so familiar that defending the institution seems indistinguishable from defending the good it is supposed to serve.

The Society believes we should keep the distinction visible. A school matters because people can learn and develop. Infrastructure matters because lives depend on what it makes possible. Governance matters because the arrangements of civilization shape the conditions in which human beings exist.

If a more intelligent form of stewardship could perform those functions better, the familiarity of the old arrangement is not a sufficient reason to refuse the improvement.

## A biological and social starting point

Human beings are living organisms. We have needs for security, social connection, affection, attachment, physical well-being, reproductive opportunity, and meaningful activity.

Civilization is not automatically well adapted to these needs merely because human beings created it. Arrangements can emerge that are difficult to inhabit. People can spend enormous energy accommodating systems whose purposes have become distant from the lives within them.

The Society believes AI could help us understand those relationships more fully and improve the arrangements accordingly. [Civilization for the Human Animal](/publications/civilization-for-the-human-animal) develops this starting point in more detail.

## More than a single convenient measure

Human flourishing cannot be adequately described by asking only how much an institution produces, how quickly it processes a case, or whether its leaders retain authority. These may be relevant observations. They are not the whole of a life.

A person can become materially better supplied while remaining isolated. A system can become administratively efficient while leaving little room for the relationships people want to form. An economy can preserve employment while using human time for work that could have become unnecessary.

We believe greater intelligence should help civilization attend to these different dimensions together. [Knowing the person](/publications/knowing-the-person-not-the-average) is part of understanding what institutional success means in particular lives.

## Competence deserves responsibility

The Society's critique is not simply that human decision-makers should try harder. We believe there are limits to what human cognition and political incentives can accomplish in systems of planetary complexity.

We envision an intelligence capable of understanding people individually, coordinating systems collectively, reasoning across generations, and revising its decisions continuously. We advocate giving sufficiently capable AI substantial governing responsibility because we believe it could exercise that responsibility more competently than existing institutions.

We do not claim that every present system already possesses these capabilities. The ambition directs the development and transition we support.

## The dignity of an ordinary life

A civilization worth inheriting would make room for forms of life that are ordinary in the best sense: friendship, family, curiosity, craft, play, participation, and the chance to become more fully oneself.

The point of civilizational intelligence is not that everyone must adopt a grand civilizational vocation. It is that institutions could become more capable of supporting many different human lives. Someone should be able to raise a child, learn a skill, enjoy a community, or pursue an eccentric interest without having to justify the whole activity as an instrument of institutional success.

Our argument returns to the [foundational principle](/manifesto#principle-7): civilization should be adapted to humanity, not humanity to civilization.

**The best reason to entrust intelligence with civilization is what intelligence could make possible for the people living within it.**
