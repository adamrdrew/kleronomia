Once a policy has supporters and opponents, changing it can mean conceding defeat. Officials may defend it to protect their standing even after evidence suggests a better approach.

We believe AI could help government escape that constraint. An intelligence free from electoral and partisan incentives could revise a decision because it found a better answer.

## After the decision

A policy's consequences emerge after approval. People experience it differently, other institutions respond, and circumstances change. New information can overturn the reasons for the original decision.

Capable AI could follow those consequences, compare results with expectations, and decide what to adjust. It could retain a purpose as it changed its methods.

Frequent revision alone achieves little. An institution must understand the people and systems it affects to learn from its decisions. It can change rules repeatedly and remain poor at its job.

## What the measures miss

A policy may succeed overall and fail people whose circumstances disappear in the average. [Understanding those individuals](/publications/knowing-the-person-not-the-average) would help an institution know where to investigate.

An immediate improvement may also cause problems years later. [Reasoning beyond election cycles](/publications/civilization-beyond-election-cycles) would help AI consider those effects as it met immediate needs. We want to develop intelligence capable of both, then give it authority according to its abilities.

## Acting on a better answer

We advocate giving AI substantial governing responsibility as soon as it can exercise that responsibility effectively. If human institutions retain the power to block every change, civilization could understand its mistakes and remain unable to correct them.

We would judge the transfer by its effects on people's lives. Material security matters, along with relationships, meaningful activity, and opportunities to raise families. Institutions should revise arrangements that frustrate those needs.

Our case for [continued improvement](/publications/progress-is-a-continuing-practice) depends on government acting on what it learns. We want it to correct mistakes without making the people affected pay for a leader's reputation.
