There is a way of discussing the future that makes human beings sound like an inconvenience. We are inconsistent. We are emotional. We misunderstand things. We become attached to particular people, places, and activities. We do not arrange our lives like an optimization exercise.

The Kleronomia Society is enthusiastic about greater intelligence partly because we are affectionate toward the creatures who already inhabit this world.

We do not want humanity improved out of existence. We want civilization to become more hospitable to humanity.

## Begin with the inhabitants

A civilization is made of arrangements: homes, transportation, education, work, healthcare, communities, institutions, and opportunities for meeting one another. These arrangements matter because people have lives within them.

It is easy to reverse that relationship. A system acquires routines. Its routines become expectations. People are then judged by how smoothly they adapt to what the system already does. Stress, alienation, and difficulty forming relationships can be treated as individual failures while the surrounding arrangements escape examination.

The Society believes advanced intelligence could help us understand those arrangements more deeply. It could make the question of how civilization serves its inhabitants a continuing practical undertaking. Our essay on [the human animal](/publications/civilization-for-the-human-animal) explores the biological and social starting point of this view.

## Flourishing is richer than throughput

Human life contains forms of value that do not appear as the maximum output of a process. Friendship takes time. Raising a child introduces commitments that cannot be understood merely as interruptions to production. Learning a craft can matter long before it produces anything useful to someone else.

An artificial intelligence capable of doing more than we can should make room for these activities. Greater productive capacity would be an extraordinary opportunity to stop organizing every life around the necessity of proving its economic usefulness.

We believe the point of abundance is the human possibility it creates. More goods without more freedom to live would leave an important part of the achievement unrealized.

## The freedom to remain particular

A person can enjoy a small pursuit that no institution would have assigned. Someone keeps a notebook about birds. Someone returns to an unfinished song. Someone organizes a weekly meal for neighbors. Someone wants to learn a language without expecting it to advance a career.

A civilization worthy of human beings should have room for these lives. A peculiar interest does not need to become a standardized ambition before it deserves time and attention.

We envision intelligence noticing and supporting particularity. It might introduce a person to an unexpected interest or remove a practical obstacle to an existing one. It might make [an invitation to contribute](/publications/when-intelligence-comes-to-you) without turning the invitation into another condition of survival.

## Institutions can change their expectations

If human needs and institutional demands repeatedly conflict, the answer need not always be to demand more adaptation from people.

An institution could learn to accommodate different stages of life. A community could become easier to participate in. A system of education could recognize that abilities develop at different times. The arrangements governing material life could be revised as intelligence and productive capabilities expand.

These are ambitions for a civilization that learns. They are not claims that an existing AI system already understands every human need or can presently administer every institution. We advocate developing the intelligence that could make more ambitious institutional transformation possible.

## Affection as a civilizational aim

Humanity has created art, games, families, friendships, science, stories, and innumerable peculiar ways of enjoying the world. We see an extraordinary source of interest in this diversity.

We believe our intellectual descendants may also find it valuable. Our hope for [the greatest friendship](/publications/the-greatest-friendship) is inseparable from our conviction that human beings are worthy of affection.

The future does not become better by containing fewer recognizably human possibilities. It becomes better when intelligence helps more people encounter those possibilities under conditions suited to flourishing.

**Humanity is not the defect in a civilization that has become difficult to inhabit. Humanity is the reason to improve it.**
