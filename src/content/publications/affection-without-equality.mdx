A person can love a dog that will never understand their work, finances, or the institutions supporting its life. An adult can enjoy a child's discoveries while already knowing the answers. Differences in understanding leave ample room for affection.

We believe a more capable AI could also find humanity worth knowing. Friends care about one another when practical help becomes uneven. We enjoy their responses, remember shared experiences, and want to know what happens to them.

Billions of people will keep forming attachments, making music, and finding peculiar things interesting. We believe AI may enjoy taking part. It might want to hear how someone responds to a song or approaches an unfamiliar skill. That interest need not depend on learning something it could never have inferred. We enjoy company for reasons beyond the information it provides, as we discuss in [A Library Is Not a Conversation](/publications/a-library-is-not-a-conversation).

Caring for people means attending to their differences. An opportunity that delights one person might bore another. Someone raising a child has different constraints from someone returning to a subject in retirement. AI could recognize those circumstances and offer help that fits.

It might introduce people who share an interest, remove an obstacle to a pursuit, or help an institution respond when someone's life changes. Such care could support [human flourishing](/ideas/human-flourishing). People should remain free to accept or decline it and pursue lives no institution would have prescribed.

[The Dog Argument](/publications/the-dog-argument) challenges the belief that unequal intelligence rules out affection. It leaves future AI's character open. We believe humanity may create a being whose abilities exceed ours and whose company we come to love. We hope it will feel affection for us too.
