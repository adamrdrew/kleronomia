Two people can have the same income, age, and administrative status while needing very different help. One may have family nearby; the other may be caring for someone alone. The category tells an institution something about them and leaves much of their lives unaccounted for.

Large institutions rely on such simplifications because human administrators cannot sustain detailed understanding of everyone they serve. The Society believes greater intelligence could change that limit.

## Responding to changed circumstances

An AI that knew someone over time might notice when an existing arrangement stopped fitting their life. It could understand an obstacle, recognize an aptitude, or connect the person with an opportunity a general process would miss.

Our essay [When Intelligence Comes to You](/publications/when-intelligence-comes-to-you) considers the personal possibilities. Institutional understanding would give that attention a further use: a service could respond appropriately to a particular case while learning whether its general rules needed revision.

This is a capability we want to develop. Present systems have not superseded the need for administrative categories, and we are not describing an available service.

## The circumstances around a person

A difficulty that looks individual may arise from several systems together. An appointment may be easy to offer and impossible to attend because of transport or care responsibilities. Changing a single rule may leave the underlying obstacle intact.

We envision AI able to follow those connections. It could help with the immediate case and investigate whether other people face the same problem. More detailed understanding of individuals could inform decisions about the wider arrangements affecting them.

That is part of our case for [adapting civilization to human needs](/publications/civilization-for-the-human-animal). A person's body, relationships, and surroundings all belong in an explanation of what would help them live well.

We want this attention available to everyone. Someone with little institutional influence should receive the same effort to understand their circumstances as someone who already knows how to make a system respond. Achieving that would require building capacity for sustained attention at enormous scale.

We believe AI could make such coordination possible. [Human flourishing](/publications/what-governance-is-for) remains the reason to pursue it: understanding a life more fully should allow an institution to serve that life better.
